Revised 18 Sep — the indicator’s own panel now measures the same way

There is a small tendency for swept levels to be followed rather than faded, worth roughly 3–4pp. It keeps the same sign under stratification, non-overlap and split-half. It is also smaller than the data feed's disagreement with itself, worth about +0.02R a trade, and would take the better part of a decade of 30-minute history to prove.

The stats panel inside the indicator has since been given drift control and a locally measured baseline, and the poor-extreme hypothesis it reports now reads null on two instruments at four bar sizes. At one day the pooled sample is 2,079 extremes with an MDL of 2.5pp — a third of the noise floor, and the best-powered measurement anywhere in this record. It returns −0.7pp. Getting there required correction 16, which had been deflating every poor-revisit number in this log.

More importantly it is not stable. It varies by instrument (τ = 4.4pp) and within an instrument across time. So a full-sample number for any single chart is an average over regimes that will not repeat — there is no fixed quantity underneath it for that number to estimate.

Reading your own stats panel

Two identities hold by construction, and both are worth checking before you trust a screenshot:

  • Total R = (R win + R loss) + (R expired + R flat). Every LG_REALR write is paired with a bucket write, so a panel that does not reconcile is a misread, not a bug. Gold: 178.7 − 137 − 0.6 = +41.1R. ✓
  • Total R ÷ all signals = Expectancy. Gold: 41.1 / 371 = 0.11R. ✓
  • Realised average win can legitimately exceed the 2R cap. Fills happen at the next bar's open and R is recomputed from the price you actually got, so an adverse open stretches the realised multiple. The minimum-R filter then discards favourable gaps while keeping adverse ones. The average is biased upward by construction.
  • Bar-derived stats — balance break held, CME gaps filled, poor extreme revisited — do not depend on intrabar data. If two runs report these identically, they saw the same bars, and any difference in trade count is coming from settings, not the feed.

Trap Engine — the second hypothesis, tested properly

A different claim: a level defended twice has resting stops beyond it; price trades through, triggers them, fails to hold, and closes back inside; the traders filled on the break are offside and must exit. Forced flow, which has a deadline — unlike "price away from value returns to value", which has none.

Fixed 2R target, so break-even is exactly 33.3% everywhere. Same ledger as above: confirmed bars, next-bar-open fills, stop tested before target.

This one was a controlled experiment

Unlike every Auction Cipher cell on this page, the Trap Engine was tested with a control arm: three charts run twice, identical in every respect except one toggle. That is the difference between an observation and an experiment, and it is why the result below can be believed.

Positioning Engine — the third hypothesis

The only one that reads a data source the other two never touched: open interest. Not price, not volume — how many contracts are actually open, and whether they are being closed by force. A long flush is open interest collapsing on a down bar while longs were crowded: contracts destroyed, not created, and somebody's position closed for them.

The magnet test — asking where, not which way

After three hypotheses about direction read zero, this one asks about destination: does price return to structurally-identified levels more than to ordinary ones? Every magnet was registered with a mirror — same distance, same birth price, same instant, opposite side — so each level is its own control.

The finding that reframes everything above

The mirror baseline is 76%. Any level near price gets revisited about three quarters of the time over 200 bars.

Which means Auction Cipher's Poor high revisited 80% — printed on its panel all along, and quoted as encouraging earlier in this session — was 80% against 76%. Nothing. That stat had never had a baseline, and neither had the reasoning built on it.

Round one: the classifier was broken

The first build classified 23 extremes POOR out of 1,830 — 1.3%. It compared volume in the extreme row against the POC row, which asks whether a distribution's tail is thinner than its mode. The answer is always yes. It measured the shape of a distribution and called it the shape of an auction.

Replaced with the textbook definition as a count: how many bars topped within one profile row of the period high. One bar spiking and being rejected is a tail (EXCESS); three or more topping at the same price is an auction that stalled (POOR). The POOR share then landed at 35–72% across instruments, and the panel now marks itself CLASSIFIER BROKEN below 10%.

Poor extremes are revisited 63.5% of the time; excess extremes 68.9%. Auction theory says an unfinished auction is revisited more than a finished one. The only significant result on this page points the other way, and both sides agree in sign (highs −7.3pp, lows −3.4pp).

Prior POC, VAH, VAL and session gaps all landed within 2pp of their mirrors with signs split across instruments. Session gaps were the cleanest kill of all: 86.7% filled against 86.7% for the mirror — lift exactly zero on 287 pairs. "Gaps get filled" is true and entirely uninformative.

Two limits I did not design around

The mirror does not control for direction. Magnets above price read −1.3pp, magnets below read +3.2pp. Every instrument in the sample was falling, and a level below a falling market is reached more often than one the same distance above whatever it means. So the mirror test is distance-matched but trend-exposed.

The poor-vs-excess test is direction-matched but not distance-matched. It avoids the trend problem and is the stronger of the two, but poor and excess levels may sit at systematically different distances from price.

Neither test supports the hypothesis. One reads null, the other reads backwards. There is no test here under which poor extremes behave like magnets.

And MAE/D ran 0.5 to 3.1 throughout — the distance price travelled the wrong way before reaching the level, as a multiple of the distance to it. Even a real lift would have needed a stop wider than its target.

The regime test — taking the one significant result seriously

The magnet run produced the only two-sigma result in the project: poor extremes revisited less than excess ones, backwards from theory. Rather than discard it, this file took it as a mechanism — a poor high means the period closed near its high, so price was still going; an excess high means a spike that was slammed back, so the market then ranges. Extreme quality as a regime classifier, read upside down.

Both extremes vote; only unanimity counts. Resolution is a symmetric race, plus and minus 1 ATR, so the arithmetic null is 50% and no stop, target or cost assumption enters the measurement.

Why this is NOT flipped again

A −6.4pp edge inverts to +6.4pp, and that is the obvious move. It is not available. This hypothesis was already an inversion of auction theory; flipping once more lands back near the original reading, which four prior measurements had put at zero. The chain is dozens of tests, then one −2.25, then a hypothesis built on it, then a −1.90 — each link a fresh degree of freedom. Inverting a losing rule only pays when the losing is systematic, and at z −1.90 with one instrument dissenting it is not established.

The two near-significant results contradict each other

The magnet test said poor highs are revisited less, which implies price carries through them. The regime test says calling continuation on a poor high is wrong, which implies price turns back. Both sit near two sigma and they point in incompatible directions.

That is not a puzzle needing a cleverer theory. Two near-two-sigma results that cannot both be true is the signature of many tests run against no signal — which is exactly what this page has been recording all along.

The session test — the clock, and an accidental replication

Five hypotheses all derived a view from price and volume structure and every one was blind to the time of day. This asks a different kind of question — a calendar fact rather than a pattern — and finally measures IB ext, which Auction Cipher has printed on its dashboard all along without anyone checking whether it means anything.

The instrument validated itself

The volatility profile peaks at 12:00–15:00 UTC on all four instruments — the London afternoon / New York morning overlap — and troughs in the Asian small hours. Textbook, and identical across metals and crypto. The clock is wired correctly, so everything below can be read.

IB extension rates came back 56–80%, all clear of the IB TOO SHORT guard. The initial balance was a real balance, not a formality.

Directional seasonality: nothing

Across eight three-hour blocks, five had metals and crypto pointing in opposite directions. The two largest apparent effects — 21:00–24:00 (metals +5.8, crypto −3.2) and 15:00–18:00 (metals −4.2, crypto +2.9) — are exactly the ones that disagree most. And gold/silver are one bet, as are BTC/ETH, so four instruments is really two independent observations.

The excursion test — and the answer

Six hypotheses all asked whether this entry is a good entry, and all read zero. That result is consistent with two different worlds. Either there is no money here at this timescale, or there is money and it is not in the entry — it is in the shape of the distribution, where losses cut at a fixed size and winners that occasionally run very far pay for everything. The second is the oldest claim in trading, and if true it explains all six nulls at once.

Entries every ten bars on a timer, no view at all, long and short from the same bar, stop at 1 ATR. Then: how far did each run before the stop took it?

Why this one needs no baseline

For a driftless random walk with a stop at −1R, the probability of touching +nR first is exactly 1/(1+n) — gambler's ruin, arithmetic, not estimated. Two earlier tests were wrecked by getting a measured baseline wrong. This one has nothing to get wrong. And drift is cancelled by construction rather than by correction, because every entry is taken both ways at the same distance.

What this settles

There is no fat right tail. "Cut losses, let winners run" has an expectancy of zero here — not small, not marginal, zero to three decimal places at every target from 1R to 12R.

So it is the first world, not the second. The six hypotheses found nothing because there is nothing at this timescale to find, not because their entries were poor. On stop-anchored excursions, 30-minute gold, silver, Bitcoin and Ethereum are indistinguishable from a coin flip with a ruler.

This is not another null on the pile. It is the result that explains the pile.

Setup excursion — the objection, tested

The excursion test entered on a timer, and the obvious objection was raised: a timer is not a setup. Nobody waits for a bar count. They wait for a swing failure, a sweep, an inducement. That objection was correct, and the conclusion drawn from the timer run — that it explained the six nulls — was too strong. The unconditional distribution being random-walk means subsets must average to it, not that every subset is random.

So the same machinery was pointed at conditioned entries, with the timer running in the same bars to supply a baseline blended to each bucket's own long/short mix.

A mechanism that fits the shape

The SFP row is worst at the near targets and least bad at the far one. That is what entries killed by noise look like, rather than entries wrong about direction. A swing failure puts you at a local extreme with a stop just beyond it — precisely where the market has just demonstrated it can trade. The obvious stop is where the next wick goes.

The green cell, and why it was not kept

+HTF on gold reads +10.4 at 5R, the only green row produced all session. It is also N=61, against −5.5, −10.7 and −9.1 on the same condition elsewhere — a 21-point spread where the detectable edge is ±4.7pp.

Twenty cells, one instrument pops. That is the identical shape as the +5.1pp at z 2.25 defended at length earlier in this same log, and the IB replication had already shown per-instrument splits reversing on a single changed input. Banking it would have been repeating the day's first mistake at the end of the day.

Not tested: inducement — the two-stage grab where price takes minor liquidity first and only then sweeps the real level. Swing failure and liquidity sweep were folded together here because at 30 minutes they are the same event. The two-stage version remains open, and defining it mechanically means choices that become free parameters.

Edge Lab — one harness, and the mirror

Nine hypotheses had each brought their own baseline, and two had been wrecked by getting it wrong. So the tenth brought none. Every signal opens its own opposite — when a swept high fires a short, a long opens on the same bar, at the same distance, with the same stop.

The mirror shares the entry bar, so it shares the drift, the volatility regime, the session, the news and the position in the trend. Anything that is a property of the moment rather than the signal helps both sides equally and cancels in the difference. LIFT* = signal − mirror is the only column on the panel that is hard to fake.

Two corrections that changed nothing, and one that mattered

The raw lift cancels drift only when a bucket is direction-balanced. A bucket that is mostly short signals has a mirror set that is mostly long, so a trending sample separates them with no signal present: raw LIFT = signal + skew × drift. LIFT* removes it by computing the lift separately inside the long-signal and short-signal strata and averaging — under pure drift the two are equal and opposite, so it is exactly zero at any mix.

Simulation confirmed it: drift θ = 0.5 into a 90/10 skewed bucket with nothing planted gives a raw lift of −25.6pp and a LIFT* of 0.0. Then the L/S column showed every 30-minute sweep bucket was already balanced — gold 152/150, ETH 221/217 — so across all fourteen it changed nothing, and this page recorded it as a guarantee rather than a repair.

The 4-hour run corrected that. See the timescale section below: at 4h the drift is +10.9pp and the bucket is skewed 83/106, and the correction removes exactly the artefact the algebra predicts.

Independence and persistence — the last two things that were wrong

The trades were never independent

Sweeps arrive about 28 bars apart while a trade can live 200, so several are always open on the same stretch of price. The binomial standard error counts them as separate observations. They are not.

The two direction-arms inside a single instrument measure the damage: under independence they should disagree by exactly sampling error, Q/df = 1. Measured across fourteen instruments: 1.68. That is a 1.3× standard-error understatement applied to every z on this page — and it is precisely what drags the fixed-effect pooled z from −3.12 to −2.40, back under the bar it appeared to clear.

The fix is construction, not a factor: a second tally admits only a chain of trades that never overlap, so the next one starts after the last is fully resolved. Those are independent by definition and their z needs no correction. N collapses — which is the point, and is what the nominal N had been borrowing against.

Why these columns are not pooled

Bar sizes on one instrument are the same price history resampled, not independent samples of it. They can legitimately be tested for agreement, which is what Q does here. They must not be pooled for significance, which would count the same days three times over.

One caveat that cuts against reading too much into the ladder itself: the four charts are four different experiments, not four views of one. The stop is 1 ATR of that timeframe and the horizon is 200 bars of that timeframe, so bar size, stop size and holding window all move together — 50 hours at 15m against 800 at 4h. Nothing is held fixed. The inversion between 15m and 30m replicates on both gold and Bitcoin (combined z 3.95), but what it establishes is that the sign of the answer is set by the parameterisation, not that the market changes character at 30 minutes. Either way it is fatal: an effect that inverts when you change a chart setting is a parameter choice, not an edge.

The test of that explanation is simple: on a periods axis, daily and weekly land on the same point. The days axis said they should differ by 4pp; the periods axis says they should coincide, and they do.

So the honest statement is narrower than the one published: survival follows T−0.32 in the horizon measured in periods, and days is the right proxy only while a period is a day. The law is real, it predicted three times, and its domain is the sub-daily regime. Stating a domain is not a weakening — a law without one is just a curve that has not yet met its counter-example.

The tooltip now separates the two cases: a starved count at a low bar size is the chart, and a starved count at an hour or above, where the chart really does hold hundreds of periods, is genuinely the input. Every guidance string this project ships is a claim, and this one had not been tested against the range of charts it would be read on.

This test gains power going up the timeframes. The sweep test lost it.

The 4-hour cells carry 508 and 411 poor extremes against 98 and 108 at 15 minutes — and the pooled MDL falls to 4.8pp, finally below the noise floor. That is the first properly-powered reading of this hypothesis in the entire project: a sample that could comfortably have seen a 6.4pp effect, returning −1.5.

The contrast with the timescale section above is exact and it is not a contradiction. The sweep test’s unit of observation is a bar, so raising the bar size buys nothing and loses chart history — one sweep per thirty bars either way. This test’s unit is a period, and 200 bars spans sixteen times as many periods at 4 hours as at 15 minutes. Which direction a timeframe ladder helps depends on what the denominator counts.

Stated exactly

A poor extreme is revisited about as often as any level the same distance away — if anything very slightly less, consistently, on two unrelated instruments at four bar sizes. At the one rung where the sample can resolve an effect worth acting on, it reads −1.5pp. A real null, not an underpowered one, replicated eight times.

The claim that a poor high is unfinished business the market must return to is not supported. The reason it survived this long is that every earlier test of it scored against an imported constant, using a watcher that invented misses.

That is not the tool failing to find something; it is the tool finally agreeing with its own evidence.

The pattern in the list

Correction 17 comes in two halves that are the same mistake. A proportion sitting exactly on 0 or 1 makes its own variance vanish, so the panel read five trades as infinitely precise; and a fitted law with no stated domain reads every extrapolation as equally trustworthy, so a curve that had predicted three rungs to within a point missed the fourth by six sigma. Both are a confidence that was never measured, only inherited from a formula that had no way to say “not here”.

Correction 16 is the only one here that no reading of a panel could have exposed. Its numbers looked entirely plausible — a poor high revisited half the time is a perfectly believable result. It surfaced because the fix for correction 15 required the single-slot watcher to become an array, and the number moved 12.5pp when it did. Some measurement errors are invisible from the output and only fall out of unrelated repairs.

Corrections 1, 3, 4 and 7 are about the denominator — what a number is being compared against, and how many chances it had. Corrections 9 and 10 are about what counts as one observation. Correction 11 is about whether the thing being measured belongs to the market or to the chart. Corrections 12 through 15 are the panel caught making the exact mistake it was built to prevent — four times, in four consecutive passes, each time in a different part of the same file — the last of them one run after the fix that was supposed to settle it. All three were written before corrections 7 and 10 existed, and none was revisited when they did. A standard added late does not propagate backwards through the code that preceded it. That is the most transferable thing this project produced, and it cost three rounds of finding it the hard way. Not one of the seventeen is about the trading logic. Every single result this project lost, it lost to measurement rather than to a bad idea about markets.

Where this actually lands

The answer is not “no edge”. It is narrower, and more useful:

The finding, stated exactly

There is a small tendency for swept levels to be followed rather than faded — roughly 3–4pp at a 2R target. It keeps the same sign under direction-stratification, under non-overlapping sampling, and on three of five instruments across both halves of their charts.

It is also smaller than the 6.4pp disagreement between two feeds on the same asset, worth about +0.02R a trade against a 0.035R CFD cost, and would need roughly 4,100 sweeps — about nine years of 30-minute history — to establish at the standard this record enforces.

And the day ended on a shape that is not coincidence. Where the effect was large enough to pay for its venue, it did not persist; where it persisted, the venue ate it.

If anything is ever tested again

  1. Fix the configuration and the rung before looking

    The target rung, the stop width, the instrument list and the dates, all written down first. Correction 8 exists because a verdict that picks its own best cell is not a test.

  2. Budget the sample from the effect you would act on

    Below 1,600 trades a 6.4pp effect is found less than half the time. Below 400 the test cannot separate anything from feed noise and should not be run.

  3. Report the non-overlapping number alongside the nominal one

    They differ by a factor of 1.3 in the standard error here. An effect that lives only in the nominal column is overlap.

  4. Cut the sample in half before believing any of it

    The cheapest correction on the list and the one that killed the most. Two of the five largest effects measured did not survive it.
